The math

One petal shape, repeated. All that changes is where each copy sits and which way it points.

One petal

A petal starts narrow, fattens in the middle, narrows to a point. That's a sine wave:

width ( u ) = maxWidth ⋅ sin ( π u )

u is how far along the petal you are, 0 at the base to 1 at the tip. It also curls into a shallow trough, more so near the tip.

Where each petal goes

Each petal needs two numbers: how far out, and which way it faces. They come from unrelated rules.

Distance comes from a spiral, drawn in the diagram above. Each step out is the same size, plus a sine wave that makes it bulge and pinch as it turns — fading outward, so the lobes sit near the middle.

r ( θ ) = ( 1 + θ ) ⋅ ( 1 + 0.3 ⋅ e − 0.15 θ ⋅ sin ( n θ ) )

It's read outermost-first, so petal one is the big open one at the rim and the last is the bud at the center. Then everything scales so the outer petal matches your bloom size.

Which way it points

Direction ignores the spiral entirely. Each petal just turns a fixed amount further than the last:

angle i = ( i + n ) ⋅ 137.5 °

Why 137.5°? Turn by 120° and petal 4 lands on petal 1, petal 5 on petal 2, forever — three spokes and dead space. Any clean fraction of a circle does that. 137.5° isn't one, so no petal repeats an earlier direction; each drops into the biggest gap left.

The number comes from the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2, the most stubbornly irrational number there is — the hardest to approximate with any fraction. Divide a full turn by φ² and you get 137.507…°, so it resists lining up better than any other angle. Hence the name: the golden angle.

Plants do this for real; the arrangement is called phyllotaxis. A sunflower head packs its seeds at roughly this angle, and the spiral arms you can count in one are almost always consecutive Fibonacci numbers — 34 one way, 55 the other. That's not a coincidence: ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers converge on φ. Pinecones, pineapples and the leaves up a stem do the same thing, which is what stops higher leaves from shading the ones below.

(The + n turns the whole arrangement as you change windings, so the outer petal moves too instead of staying put.)
