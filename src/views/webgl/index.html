The math

One petal

Every petal is the same shape, copied over and over. Narrow, wide, narrow again: a sine wave.

width ( u ) = maxWidth ⋅ sin ( π u )

u tracks position along the petal: 0 at the base, 1 at the tip.

The spiral

A real rose doesn't grow all its petals at once. Each new one forms a bit further out than the last, tracing a spiral as the bud opens. The simplest version is the Archimedean spiral: every step outward is the same size.

r ( θ ) = 1 + θ

That's also the order they grow in: tight inner turns are the bud, big open petals at the rim are the oldest.

The golden angle

Distance out comes from the spiral. Spacing is a separate rule: each new petal turns a fixed amount further around than the last one.

angle i = i ⋅ 137.5 °

137.5° isn't arbitrary. A clean fraction like 120° makes every third petal overlap an earlier one: three spokes, wasted gaps. 137.5° never repeats, so each petal always lands in the biggest gap left.

That angle comes from the golden ratio, φ = (1+√5)/2, the number fractions approximate worst. A full turn divided by φ² is 137.507…°. Nothing ever lines up with it.

Real plants use this angle too. It's called phyllotaxis: leaves, seeds and petals spaced so none shades or crowds the one before it. Each gets its own share of sunlight or space, packed as efficiently as possible.

(In this model, changing the winding count also spins the whole arrangement, so the outer petal moves instead of staying fixed in place.)
